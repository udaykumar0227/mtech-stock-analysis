## Abstract

Stock markets alternate between distinct behavioural phases — sustained
trends, quiet ranges and turbulent, high-volatility episodes — and no single
trading rule performs well across all of them. This dissertation develops a
data-science framework that first *classifies the prevailing market style* from
historical price data, then *selects the analysis strategy appropriate to that
style*, and finally *back-tests the resulting signals* against a buy-and-hold
baseline. Trend is quantified as a normalised fast-versus-slow moving-average
spread confirmed by the MACD histogram; volatility is quantified as the
percentile rank of realised volatility within a trailing window, making the
"high-volatility" definition stock-relative rather than absolute. The two axes
are combined into an interpretable market-style label. Signals are generated by
momentum rules in trending regimes, mean-reversion rules in quiet ranges, and
position de-risking in high-volatility regimes. Evaluation uses a vectorised
back-test with one-bar position lagging and basis-point transaction costs,
reporting CAGR, annualised volatility, Sharpe and Sortino ratios, maximum
drawdown and hit rate. The framework is implemented as a reproducible Python
package with a synthetic multi-regime data generator, a command-line interface
and unit tests.

**Keywords:** market regime, stock analysis, trend detection, volatility
percentile, momentum, mean-reversion, back-testing, quantitative finance.

## Scope decisions (be ready to defend these in the viva)

1. **Rule-based, not machine-learned.** Regime labelling is unsupervised
   labelling by *economic definition* — trend (normalised fast/slow moving-average
   spread, confirmed by MACD) crossed with volatility (percentile rank of
   realised volatility). It is chosen deliberately for interpretability and
   reproducibility, not as a substitute for a model. Success is therefore
   measured by back-test performance and risk-adjusted metrics, not by
   prediction accuracy. This is a methodology contribution, not an ML one.

2. **Long-only (`allow_short: false`).** SEBI does not permit Indian retail
   investors to short-sell for delivery, so a long/short daily strategy would
   not be executable. Signals are `{0, +1}` — long, hold or exit. A short leg
   exists behind a flag for completeness but is off by default.

3. **Stock-relative volatility.** "High volatility" is a *percentile rank*
   within a trailing window, so a single threshold applies across instruments
   instead of an absolute value tuned to one price scale.

4. **Data provenance and quality.** Free APIs provide no IPO/listing dates, so
   those come from a hand-maintained reference file and are indicative only.
   Daily returns above `max_abs_return` are masked as likely corporate-action
   artifacts and the excluded count is disclosed — not silently dropped.

## 1. Outline of chapters (to be written)

1. **Introduction** — motivation, problem statement, objectives, scope,
   contributions, dissertation organisation.
2. **Literature Review** — regime-switching models (Markov switching),
   technical-indicator studies, momentum vs. mean-reversion anomalies,
   limitations of prior work.
3. **Methodology** — data description; feature engineering formulae
   (returns, SMA/EMA, MACD, RSI, ATR, Bollinger, realised volatility);
   regime-classification rules; signal-generation logic; evaluation metrics.
4. **Implementation** — software architecture, module design, configuration,
   reproducibility, testing strategy.
5. **Results and Discussion** — regime composition per instrument, signal
   activity, performance tables, comparison with buy-and-hold, sensitivity to
   `trend_threshold` and `vol_high_pct`.
6. **Conclusion and Future Work** — findings, limitations, extensions
   (intraday data, multi-asset portfolios, ML-based regime prediction).
7. **References** and **Appendices** (code listings, extra plots).
